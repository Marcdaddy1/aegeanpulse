## What changed from the Monday brief, and why

The brief's posts were well researched but not safe to publish as written:

- **Removed invented practices.** The brief had Marcus say "we quote on depth now,
  not on feature count". AegeanPulse sells fixed packages, so that isn't true.
  Replaced with what *is* true: Builder is priced as one complete workflow.
- **Removed claims the paper doesn't make.** "No model cleared 50%", "second place
  was 28.8%" and "Microsoft Research" are not stated on the paper's page. The
  paper names two agents and their scores; that's what we quote.
- **Removed "layers".** The paper says *restoration depth*. The brief turned that
  into "layers of logic" and built a pricing method on it. We describe the finding
  in the paper's own terms.
- **Links now point at aegeanpulse.com**, with UTMs, instead of arXiv.

AI writes code now. So why hasn't the cost of building software collapsed?

A research benchmark published last week gives the clearest answer I've seen.

It's called ProgramDistill. Instead of handing coding agents a tidy spec, it gives them a working web app and asks them to rebuild parts of it. That's much closer to a real client brief.

Two of the leading agents completed 49.2% and 28.8% of the full rebuilds.

But the headline score isn't the interesting bit. The drop-off is.

Restore a small piece of a working app: the agents succeeded 100% and 96% of the time.
Restore a larger, more connected part: 64% and 32%.

That's a real build in miniature. The booking form appears in twenty minutes. Then comes the double booking, the refund, the customer in another timezone, the no-show — and that's where the agents fell down.

The screen got cheap. What happens behind it didn't.

So if you're comparing quotes, don't ask why AI hasn't made it cheaper. Ask the builder what happens when something goes wrong. That answer tells you whether they've priced the easy part or the whole job.
