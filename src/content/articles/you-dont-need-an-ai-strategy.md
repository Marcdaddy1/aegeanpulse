Somewhere in the last two years, "we need an AI strategy" became a thing small business owners say out loud, usually with a slight wince, the way people talk about going to the gym. It sounds responsible and slightly overdue. It is also, for most small businesses, the wrong goal — and chasing it is a reliable way to spend money and end up with nothing running.

The idea of an AI strategy came from big companies, where it makes sense. A business with hundreds of staff, several departments, and a technology budget genuinely needs a plan for how AI fits across all of it, who owns it, and how the pieces connect. That plan justifies its own cost because there's so much to coordinate. Copy that thinking into a fifteen-person firm and it collapses. You don't have the surface area to need a strategy. You have a handful of processes and not enough hours, and what you actually need is for one of them to get easier.

## What "strategy" usually turns into

Ask for an AI strategy and here's what tends to arrive: a document. A well-formatted one, with a maturity assessment, a list of use cases ranked on a two-by-two grid, a phased roadmap stretching over eighteen months, and a slide about "building an AI-ready culture." It reads impressively. It cost real money. And three months later nothing in your business works any differently, because a roadmap is a description of work, not the work.

This isn't a knock on the people who produce these documents. For a large organisation, that artefact is genuinely useful, because the hard part there is alignment across many teams. For a small business, alignment isn't the hard part. You can align your whole company in a conversation over coffee. The hard part is doing the thing, and a strategy deck is specifically the part that isn't doing the thing.

The tell is simple. If the output of your AI investment is a document rather than a change to how work happens, you bought the wrong thing.

## One prioritised move beats a ranked list of twenty

There's a version of "strategy" that's actually worth having, and it's much smaller than the word suggests. It fits on a page. It answers one question: of everything we could do with AI, what is the single highest-value thing to do first, and what would doing it involve?

That's it. Not twenty ranked use cases. One, with enough detail that you could start on Monday. The value of prioritisation isn't the ranked list — it's the decision at the top of it. A list of twenty opportunities is a way of avoiding a choice. Naming the one you'll actually do is the choice, and it's the only part that changes anything.

This is why the useful version of strategy work is cheap and fast. You're not mapping an eighteen-month transformation. You're looking at where your team loses the most time, checking which of those a current tool can realistically handle, and picking the one with the best ratio of value to effort. A focused audit like that takes days, not months, and it ends with a recommendation you can act on rather than a plan you'll file.

## Momentum is the real asset

Here's the part the roadmap approach misses entirely. The most valuable thing a small business can build in its first year with AI isn't a plan. It's evidence. One workflow that visibly saves time does more for your AI adoption than any strategy document, because it turns an abstract idea your team is wary of into something concrete they've felt.

Strategy-first gets this backwards. It asks people to buy into a long programme before they've seen a single benefit. Enthusiasm leaks away over the months it takes to reach anything real, and the roadmap becomes another well-meant plan gathering dust. Thing-first does the opposite. You ship something small, people feel the difference, and the appetite for the next step is now real rather than hoped for. You earn the roadmap by shipping, instead of shipping because of the roadmap.

A plan built before you've done anything is a guess. A plan built after you've shipped one workflow is grounded in what you actually learned — how your team reacted, what the tools handled well, where the real friction turned out to be. That second plan is worth having. The first one rarely survives contact with reality.

## Start smaller than feels sensible

If you take one thing from this, make it this: the right first step is almost always smaller than feels appropriate for the size of the opportunity. AI feels big, so a big response feels proportionate. It isn't. The big response is what stalls.

Pick the single process that eats the most of your team's week. Ignore everything else for now — not forever, just for now. Get that one thing working properly, in the tools you already use, with a person still in the loop where judgement matters. Then, and only then, look up and ask what's next, informed by what you just learned rather than what you guessed at the start.
